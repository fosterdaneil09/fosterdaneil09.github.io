How to Write a Contract Law Assignment: Offer, Acceptance and Consideration Explained

Most students lose marks in a contract law assignment for one reason: they know the cases but not how to use them. They list Carlill, Hyde v Wrench and Currie v Misa, then never connect them to the facts in the question. This guide shows how to write a contract law assignment that applies offer, acceptance and consideration step by step, using English authorities your marker will expect to see. If you also want broader help with law assignments, such as planning and structure, that is a separate skill. Here we stay with the three elements that decide whether a contract exists at all.

A quick note on approach. Offer, acceptance and consideration are not three boxes to tick. Markers reward the student who spots which element is actually in dispute and spends most of the word count there. A problem about an advert needs real analysis of invitation to treat, not a general lecture on consideration.

Offer: What Counts and What Does Not

  • An offer shows willingness to be bound if the other side agrees. An advert can be one: in Carlill, the reward promise was a unilateral offer to the world.
  • An invitation to treat only invites offers. Shop displays and most adverts fall here: Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (Southern) Ltd [1953] 1 QB 401; Fisher v Bell [1961] 1 QB 394.
  • An offer ends by revocation, rejection, counter-offer, lapse of time or death.
  • Revocation only works once it is communicated: Byrne v Van Tienhoven (1880) 5 CPD 344.
  • A mere request for information is not a counter-offer: Stevenson, Jaques & Co v McLean (1880) 5 QBD 346.

Acceptance: Mirror Image, Silence and the Postal Rule

  • Acceptance must match the offer. Changing a term creates a counter-offer: Hyde v Wrench.
  • Silence is not acceptance: Felthouse v Bindley (1862) 11 CBNS 869.
  • Under the postal rule, acceptance by post takes effect when posted: Adams v Lindsell (1818) 1 B & Ald 681.
  • Instant methods such as telex follow the general rule, so acceptance takes effect on receipt: Entores Ltd v Miles Far East Corp [1955] 2 QB 327.
  • Conduct can accept an offer: Brogden v Metropolitan Railway Co (1877) 2 App Cas 666.

For email, say which approach you are applying and why, rather than stating the rule as settled.

Consideration: Value, Timing and Existing Duties

  • The classic definition is a benefit to one party or a detriment to the other: Currie v Misa.
  • It must be sufficient but need not be adequate. Courts do not weigh the bargain: Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestlé Co Ltd [1960] AC 87.
  • Past consideration is generally no good: Re McArdle [1951] Ch 669.
  • Doing what you are already bound to do was traditionally not enough: Stilk v Myrick (1809) 2 Camp 317. Williams v Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd [1991] 1 QB 1 softened this where there is a practical benefit.
  • Part payment of a debt does not discharge it: Foakes v Beer (1884) 9 App Cas 605. Promissory estoppel may help: Central London Property Trust Ltd v High Trees House Ltd [1947] KB 130.

How to Structure a Contract Law Assignment Answer

Spend ten minutes on a plan before you write. This walkthrough on planning a law assignment before you start writing shows one way to do it. For each issue, use the same four steps:

  • Issue: name the exact question, for example whether a reply was an acceptance.
  • Rule: state the principle with one authority.
  • Application: apply it to the facts. Most marks sit here.
  • Conclusion: answer in one sentence.

Cite every case in footnotes using OSCOLA. The Oxford quick reference guide covers the format.

A Short Contract Law Assignment Example

Sam emails Priya offering to sell his laptop for £400. Priya replies, “I’ll take it for £350.” Sam does not answer. The next day Priya writes, “Fine, £400.”

  • Issue: whether a contract exists at £400.
  • Rule: a counter-offer destroys the original offer (Hyde v Wrench), and silence cannot be acceptance (Felthouse v Bindley).
  • Application: Priya’s £350 reply was a counter-offer, so Sam’s £400 offer ended. Her later “Fine, £400” is a fresh offer, and Sam’s silence does not accept it. Had she only asked whether he would take £350, Stevenson v McLean would change the answer.
  • Conclusion: no contract has been formed.

Mistakes That Cost Marks in a Contract Law Assignment

  • Retelling the facts of a case instead of applying its principle.
  • Citing Williams v Roffey without mentioning Stilk v Myrick, which it qualifies.
  • Treating the postal rule as automatic for email.
  • Ignoring intention to create legal relations in family or social scenarios.
  • Using Australian or US cases without checking they apply in England and Wales.

FAQs

What is offer, acceptance and consideration in a contract?

An offer is a definite statement of willingness to be bound. Acceptance is unqualified agreement to it. Consideration is the value each side gives in exchange for the other’s promise.

What are the three C’s of a contract?

It is not a fixed legal test, and sources label the three C’s differently. In English law the core elements are offer, acceptance and consideration, alongside intention to create legal relations and certainty. Check which version your module uses.

What is Lord Denning’s red hand rule?

If a term is particularly onerous or unusual, the party relying on it must take reasonable steps to draw it to the other side’s attention. It comes from Spurling v Bradshaw [1956] 1 WLR 461 and was applied in Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd [1989] QB 433. It deals with incorporation of terms, not formation.

How do I answer a contract law problem question on offer and acceptance?

Work through the facts in order. Was there an offer, was it still open, was the reply an acceptance or a counter-offer, and was it communicated? Apply one authority per point and end each issue with a short conclusion.

Can a contract exist without consideration?

Generally no, but a promise made by deed is enforceable without it.
